Getting started

The planner turns a GPS route into a day-by-day estimate of distance, time, and energy, then points at the days most likely to break. It runs entirely in your browser: nothing is uploaded anywhere, and the only network traffic is map tiles.

  1. Open index.html. A synthetic sample loop loads so every control has something to act on.
  2. Click Load GPX file and pick your route. The map, profile, and tables replace the sample.
  3. Set your body weight, base pack weight, and food per day.
  4. Choose the number of days and let the planner split them, or switch to manual and click camps onto the profile.
  5. Look at which days are flagged, move camps or add a day, and save versions you want to compare.

GPX files

Any GPX with <trkpt> (track) or <rtept> (route) points works. Each point needs an <ele> tag; points without one are read as 0 m, which will make the profile useless. Exports from CalTopo, Gaia, Garmin, and most mapping tools include elevation. If the file has several track segments they are treated as one continuous line in file order. Waypoints are ignored.

Routes drawn by hand and then given DEM elevation (CalTopo does this automatically) produce cleaner results than raw watch recordings, which carry GPS noise in both position and altitude.

Reading the screen

Elevation profile

Distance runs left to right in kilometres; height is in metres. Alternating blue bands mark the days, with camps at the band edges. The fill under the line is coloured by the grade of that stretch: green under 15%, amber from 15% to 25%, rust above 25%. Hover any point to see its elevation, the local grade, and the modeled walking speed there. Steep colour is a hint about where time and energy go, not a difficulty rating by itself: a short rust section is a scramble, a long amber one is what makes a day slow.

Both this chart and the energy-rate chart below can be explored: drag to pan, scroll or pinch to zoom, and Reset zoom to snap back. Panning and zooming stay in sync between the two charts. Placing a camp is a separate, deliberate action — see Placing camps — so an ordinary drag never adds one by accident.

When a start date and time are set, two more layers can appear on this chart: a darkened band over the part of each day that falls after that day's sunset, and a light blue rain-chance area (right-hand axis) using an hourly forecast — so you can see whether a steep stretch lines up with rain, not just whether the day overall looks wet. Both come from the same clock-time model as the day table's sunset flag.

Tap Track my location in the sidebar to overlay your live GPS position: a marker on the map and a matching blue line on this chart, both showing where you actually are along the route right now. It also reports how far off-route you've wandered, if at all. Location access is requested only when you tap the button, and tapping it again stops tracking and clears both markers.

Whole-route totals and the day table

The totals row summarises the trip: distance, ascent, descent, total hours including rests, total kilocalories, the hardest day by energy, and the food mass those calories imply at 4.5 kcal per gram (a reasonable average for dried backpacking food; fatty menus reach 5 kcal/g).

The day table has one row per day:

Energy-rate chart

The lower chart shows kilocalories per hour along the route. It is the fastest way to see where a day is expensive rather than how expensive it is overall. Long plateaus above 500 kcal/h are the climbs at load; dips near the floor are gentle descents where the model says walking costs little more than standing.

Placing camps

Shift-click the elevation profile at the distance where you want to sleep. The camps setting switches to manual and a camp appears at that kilometre; the existing automatic splits are kept as starting camps so you are adjusting a plan rather than rebuilding it. Shift-clicking within about 2% of the route length of an existing camp removes it. Camps are also listed as chips in the sidebar — click a chip to remove it, or Clear camps to start over. Choosing an automatic option again discards the manual camps.

An ordinary click, drag, or scroll on the profile only pans and zooms the view — it never places a camp. The Shift key is what makes placing one a deliberate, separate action.

Camps are placed by distance, so they are not automatically snapped to water or flat ground — but the sidebar's Suggest campsites option can point you toward one. When checked, it fetches nearby streams, rivers, and lakes from OpenStreetMap and combines that with the profile's local grade to mark spots that are both reasonably flat and within about 300 m of water, shown as blue dots on the elevation profile and the map. Coverage depends on how well the area is mapped, and the lookup is skipped for routes that span a very large area (to avoid one huge query against the free public data source). Independently of that, any day whose camp lands on notably steep ground or far from any mapped water gets a note in the day table, whether the camp was placed automatically or by hand.

Look at the map marker after placing a camp and nudge it if the spot is unrealistic. A day with no route between two camps is reported as "empty day" rather than crashing the table.

A common fix for a flagged day is to move the camp before it later, not the camp after it earlier: put the climb at the start of the day when the pack is fresh and the temperature is low.

Saving and comparing

Type a name and press Save plan. The plan stores the route geometry, every input, the camp positions, and the day results, so loading it later reproduces the screen exactly even if the GPX file is gone. Plans live in your browser's local storage: they persist between visits on the same device and browser, are not synced anywhere, and are lost if you clear site data.

Load replaces the current screen with the selected plan. Compare keeps your current screen and adds a table against the selected plan: days, distance, ascent, total hours, total kcal, hardest day, longest day, and flag count, with the difference coloured green where the current plan is easier and rust where it is harder. The per-day kilocalories of both plans are listed under the table so you can see whether a version merely moved the pain rather than reducing it. Typical use is to save a conservative and an aggressive version of the same route and compare them, or to compare the same itinerary at two pack weights.

How the calculations work

Route preparation

The raw points are joined by great-circle (haversine) distance and then resampled at a fixed 50-metre spacing so that every segment carries equal weight in the sums. Elevation is then smoothed with a 5-point moving average (a 250 m window). Without smoothing, GPS jitter of a few metres per point inflates ascent totals by hundreds of metres and creates fictitious steep grades; the trade is that genuinely short, sharp steps are softened. Grade for a segment is rise over run:

slope = Δelevation / 50 m        grade % = slope × 100

Pace

Base walking speed comes from Tobler's hiking function, an empirical fit to Imhof's mountaineering data:

speed (km/h) = 6 · e^(−3.5 · |slope + 0.05|)

It peaks at 6 km/h on a gentle 5% descent, gives about 5.0 km/h on the flat, 3.5 km/h at +10%, 2.1 km/h at +25%, and also slows on steep descents (3.5 km/h at −20%). Three multipliers then adjust it:

FactorFormulaRationale
Fitness× 0.85 to 1.3Tobler's curve is an average walker; this shifts it for you.
Load× max(0.55, 1 − 0.4 · L/W)A pack at 15% of body weight slows you about 6%; at 30%, 12%. Capped so an extreme load cannot stop you. Rule-of-thumb coefficient, not from a specific study.
Altitude× max(0.6, 1 − 0.07 · (altitude − 1500 m) / 1000) above 1500 mRoughly 7% slower per 1000 m of elevation above 1500 m, reflecting reduced aerobic capacity. Below 1500 m no penalty. Also a rule of thumb.

Segment time is simply distance divided by the adjusted speed. Moving hours per day are the sum of segment times.

Energy

Energy uses the Pandolf load-carriage equation (Pandolf, Givoni & Goldman, 1977), which predicts the metabolic rate of a person walking with a load on a grade:

M (watts) = 1.5·W + 2.0·(W+L)·(L/W)² + η·(W+L)·(1.5·V² + 0.35·V·G)

W = body mass (kg)     L = load (kg)      V = speed (m/s)
G = grade (%)          η = terrain factor (1.0 paved … 1.8 brush)

The first term is the cost of standing, the second the cost of carrying the load itself, the third the cost of moving both up the slope. Pandolf's formula is only valid uphill and on the flat; for descents the Santee et al. correction is subtracted:

M ← M − η · [ (G·(W+L)·V) / 3.5  −  ((W+L)·(G−6)²) / W  +  25·V² ]     for G < 0, using |G|

This makes gentle descents cheaper than flat walking and steep descents (past roughly 10–12%) more expensive again, as the legs brake. Two guards are applied: the result is never allowed below 1.2 × the standing cost (1.8·W watts), because the correction can go negative on steep descents where the model was not fitted, and the uphill grade term uses G only when positive.

Watts are converted to kilocalories with the segment time:

kcal = M × seconds / 4184

Energy rate on the chart is kcal ÷ hours for that segment.

Shifting load

Total food is food-per-day × number of days. The load used for day k of n is the pack at the middle of that day:

L(k) = base pack + food per day × (n − k + 0.5)

So on a 5-day trip with 0.8 kg/day, day 1 is modeled at base + 3.6 kg and day 5 at base + 0.4 kg. Because load feeds both the speed penalty and the Pandolf equation, early days are slower and more expensive per kilometre than late ones — which is why an equal-distance split usually flags day 1.

Day splits and time

Automatic splits first run the whole route at full starting load, then cut it into days with equal moving time (or equal distance), then re-run each day at its own midday load. The two-pass approach means "equal effort" is judged at a consistent weight; the final days will still come out slightly quicker because they are lighter. Manual camps cut the route at the nearest resampled point to each clicked kilometre.

Total hours for a day add rests as a fraction of moving time:

total h = moving h × (1 + rest minutes per hour / 60)

Rests add no energy in this model.

A 20 km day with 1200 m of climbing therefore lands in the 3000–3500 kcal range for this hiker before rests, which matches published field measurements of loaded hiking reasonably well.

Limits and caveats

  • Walking energy only. The kcal figures cover the hours on your feet. Basal metabolism for the rest of the day, cold, and digestion add roughly 1500–2000 kcal for most adults. For food planning, add that to the walking figure; the food-weight estimate on screen uses walking kcal only.
  • No fatigue carry-over. Each day is evaluated fresh. A brutal day 2 does not slow day 3 in this model, although it will in the field.
  • Weather, sunset, and nearby water are approximate. They come from free public data (Open-Meteo, OpenStreetMap) at the route's rough location, best-effort — not snow conditions, water crossings, or navigation difficulty. Terrain factor is still one value for the whole route.
  • Live location depends on your device's GPS. Accuracy varies with signal, and the "off route" distance is a straight-line nearest-point check, not a trail-aware one.
  • Rules-of-thumb. Tobler and Pandolf–Santee are published models; the load and altitude speed penalties and the flag thresholds are sensible defaults chosen for this tool, not fitted to data. Treat relative comparisons between plans as more trustworthy than absolute values.
  • Elevation quality dominates. A noisy GPX inflates ascent and energy even after smoothing. If ascent totals look 20% higher than your mapping tool reports, re-export with DEM elevation.
  • Camps are distances, not places. Check the map marker against water and flat ground.
  • Local storage. Saved plans stay on this device and browser. Export the GPX separately if you need the route elsewhere.
This is a planning aid for comparing options and spotting weak days before you leave. It does not replace judgement about weather, snow, river levels, permits, or your own condition on the day.
